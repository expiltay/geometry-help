3.1

Conditional Statements

Class lecture video3.1 worksheet (PDF)
Big ideaAlmost every rule in math can be written as “If ____, then ____.” The part after if is the Given. The part after then is the Conclusion.

If two angles form a linear pair, then they are supplementary.

Given = what you start with (after “if”).   Conclusion = what must follow (after “then”).

Think of it as a promise: “Whenever the Given happens, the Conclusion happens too.”

Worksheet Part I

Turning a sentence into if–then form

Many sentences are secret if–then statements. Your teacher calls these implicit conditionals. Find the pattern, then rewrite it.

If the sentence looks like…Rewrite it as…Example (not from the worksheet)
A, therefore BIf A, then B.“It rained, therefore the grass is wet” → If it rained, then the grass is wet.
All X are Y
Every X is Y
Xs are Y		If something is an X, then it is a Y.“All squares are rectangles” → If a shape is a square, then it is a rectangle.
No X is Y
None of the X are Y		If something is an X, then it is not a Y.“No triangle has four sides” → If a shape is a triangle, then it does not have four sides.
When A, B
Whenever A, B		If A, then B.“When I bake, the kitchen gets messy” → If I bake, then the kitchen gets messy.
The ___ of A is BIf you have A, then its ___ is B.“The midpoint of a segment splits it in half” → If a point is the midpoint of a segment, then it splits the segment in half.
Watch out: the Given is the type of thing or the situation. The Conclusion is what is claimed about it. In “All humans are mammals,” being human is the Given. Being a mammal is the Conclusion. Don't flip them.

Your teacher's rule: even false sentences are still conditionals. In Part I you just rewrite. You don't judge true or false yet.

I.1–2 “I am, therefore I think” / “I think, therefore I am”
Hint

Use the “A, therefore B” pattern. Whatever comes before “therefore” goes after “if.”

I.3–4 “All humans are mammals” / “Every mammal is a vertebrate”
Hint

Use “If an animal is a ___, then it is a ___.” The first noun in the sentence goes in the Given.

I.5–6 “No prime is even” / “None of the powers of 4 are prime”
Hint

Use the “No X is Y” pattern: If a number is ___, then it is not ___.

I.7–9 Line intersection, angle made of rays, hiking
Hint for 7 and 8

Ask “what situation are we talking about?” For 7 the situation is two lines intersect. For 8 the situation is a figure is an angle.

Hint for 9

“When A, B” becomes “If A, then B.” The word “always” can be dropped, because “if…then” already means always.

Worksheet Part II

True or false? Find a counterexample

An if–then statement is true only if it works every single time. To show it is false, you need just one example that breaks it. That example is called a counterexample.

A counterexample must do two things at once

  1. It makes the Given true.
  2. It makes the Conclusion false.

Example: “If a number is odd, then it is prime.”

Try 9. Is 9 odd? Yes (Given true). Is 9 prime? No, 9 = 3 × 3 (Conclusion false). So the statement is false, and 9 is a counterexample.

Example: “If two numbers are even, then their sum is even.”

Every even + even is even. No counterexample exists, so it is true.

Common mistake: a number that makes the Given false is not a counterexample. For “if a number is odd, then it is prime,” the number 4 proves nothing, because 4 isn't odd.

Words from the worksheet

  • Prime: a positive whole number with exactly two factors (1 and itself). The primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13… 2 is prime, and it's the only even prime. This matters a lot!
  • Perfect square: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36… (a whole number times itself). 1 counts.
  • Supplementary: two angles whose measures add to 180°. They do not have to touch.
  • Linear pair: two angles that sit side by side and whose outer sides make a straight line.
II.1–2 Sums of primes
Hint 1

Write it as “If two primes are added, then the sum is not prime.” Now go hunting: add small primes and see if you ever get a prime.

Hint 2

For #1, try using the prime 2. For #2, the primes must be odd. What do you always get when you add two odd numbers? Can that kind of number (bigger than 2) be prime?

Check your answer

#1 is false. One counterexample is 2 + 3 = 5 (your teacher's). #2 is true: odd + odd is even, and an even number bigger than 2 is never prime.

II.3–4 Perfect squares
Hint for 3

Look for a perfect square that equals two other perfect squares added together. Try 25 and the squares below it: 1, 4, 9, 16.

Hint for 4

“Every perfect square can be made by adding perfect squares.” Test the smallest perfect square. Can you make it by adding two or more perfect squares? (The smallest perfect square is 1.)

II.5–6 Linear pairs and supplementary pairs
Hint

#5: two angles that make a straight line always add to 180°. #6 flips it around. Can you draw two angles that add to 180° but don't touch each other? Picture two separate right angles far apart.

Worksheet Part III

Converses: swap the two halves

Original: If A, then B.
Converse: If B, then A.

Example: Original: If a shape is a square, then it has four sides. True.

Converse: If a shape has four sides, then it is a square. False. A rectangle that is not a square is a counterexample.

The lesson your teacher wants you to remember: a true statement can have a false converse. You have to check each one by itself.

III.1 If x² = 1, then x = 1
Hint

Is there another number whose square is 1? Think negative.

III.2 If x = y, then |x| = |y|
Hint

|x| means “distance from zero,” so |−3| = 3. For the converse, can two different numbers have the same absolute value?

III.3 If two angles are complementary, then each is acute
Hint

Complementary means they add to 90°, and every angle is bigger than 0°. Could either one be 90° or more? For the converse, pick two acute angles like 30° and 40°. Do they add to 90°?

III.4–5 Right angle and 90°; even and not prime
Hint

#4: “right angle” is defined as an angle that measures 90°. A definition works in both directions. #5: remember 2. For its converse, is every non-prime number even? Try 9.

Worksheet Part IV

Contrapositives: swap and say “not”

Original: If A, then B.
Contrapositive: If not B, then not A.

Example: If an animal is a dog, then it is a mammal.

Contrapositive: If an animal is not a mammal, then it is not a dog.

Magic fact: a statement and its contrapositive are always both true or both false. So for each worksheet question, you write one truth value for both.

How to say “not” correctly

StatementIts “not” (negation)
Q lies on line PRQ does not lie on line PR
PQ = QRPQ ≠ QR
A and B are both trueA is false or B is false (or both)
p is not greater than qp is greater than q (two “nots” cancel)

The tricky one is and. To break “A and B,” only one part has to fail. Your teacher did this in the worked example: the contrapositive of “If a = b and b = c, then a = c” is “If a ≠ c, then a ≠ b or b ≠ c (or both).”

IV.1–3 Points P, Q, R
Hint

Draw a line with P and R on it. Try putting Q in different places: between them, off to the right past R, exactly in the middle, a little closer to P. Does the original statement always work?

IV.4 Q on line PR and PQ = QR
Hint

The Given has an and in it. When you negate it, use or: “Q is not on line PR or PQ ≠ QR.”

IV.6 p not greater than q
Hint

Are there only two choices, bigger or smaller? What if p and q are the same number?

3.2

Proof Structure (Setting Up a Proof)

Class lecture video3.2 worksheet (PDF)
Big ideaBefore you prove anything, you set it up in three steps: (1) write it as if–then, (2) draw and label a picture of the Given, (3) write “Given:” and “Prove:” using the labels from your picture.

This worksheet only asks you to set up. You don't do the proof yet.

Worked example (not on the worksheet)

Statement: “The angles of a triangle add up to 180°.”

  1. If–then: If a figure is a triangle, then the sum of its angle measures is 180°.
  2. Diagram: draw one specific triangle and name its corners A, B, C.
  3. Given: triangle ABC
    Prove: m∠A + m∠B + m∠C = 180°

See how the Given and Prove use the letters from the picture? That's what “be specific” means.

Your teacher's model (worksheet #1)

If–then: If two lines are perpendicular, then they form four right angles.

Given: Lines m and n are perpendicular with angle 1 right.

Conclusion: Angles 2, 3 and 4 are also right.

Two rules for the diagram: draw only what the Given tells you (don't mark things you are supposed to prove), and give every point, line, or angle you mention a name.

Check your answer

(From your teacher's solutions.) If two angles are vertical, then they are equal. Given: Angles 1 and 2 are vertical. Conclusion: m∠1 = m∠2.

#3 Linear pairs are supplementary
Hint

Draw a straight line with a ray coming up out of it. That makes two angles side by side. Label them 1 and 2. The Conclusion should be an equation with numbers.

Check your answer

If two angles form a linear pair, then they are supplementary. Given: ∠1 and ∠2 form a linear pair. Conclusion: ∠1 and ∠2 are supplementary (m∠1 + m∠2 = 180°).

#4 The angles of a quadrilateral add to 360°
Hint

Just like the triangle example above, but with four corners. Name them A, B, C, D going around.

#5 Right triangles (the Pythagorean Theorem)
Hint

Draw triangle ABC with the right angle at C. The legs are the two sides that touch the right angle (AC and BC). The hypotenuse is the long side across from it (AB). The Conclusion is an equation using AC, BC, and AB.

#6–7 Equal sides and equal angles
Hint

These two are converses of each other. In #6 the Given is about sides (AB = BC = CA), and the Conclusion is about angles. In #7 it is the other way around. Your diagram can be the same triangle ABC for both.

#8 Four right angles means equal diagonals
Check your answer

(From your teacher's solutions.) If a quadrilateral has four right angles, then it has equal diagonals. Given: quadrilateral ABCD has four right angles. Prove: AC = BD.

3.3

Angle Proofs

Class lecture video3.3 worksheet (PDF)Teacher's selected proofs (PDF)
Big ideaA proof is a chain of small steps from the Given to the Conclusion. Every step needs a reason, and the only reasons allowed are: Given, a definition, a postulate, or a theorem you already proved.

The table (your teacher calls it a “table proof”)

ColumnWhat goes in it
StatementOne small fact, written as words or as an equation.
FromThe line number(s) this fact came from. Line 1 is the Given, so it has no “From.”
ReasonThe rule that lets you say it. For example “Def linear pair,” “LPT,” or “Substitution.”

The secret: most steps are translations

Most lines in an angle proof turn words into numbers or numbers back into words. A definition lets you go either way. Learn this table and angle proofs get much easier.

Words↔ NumbersReason to write
∠1 and ∠2 are supplementarym∠1 + m∠2 = 180°Def supplementary
∠1 and ∠2 are complementarym∠1 + m∠2 = 90°Def complementary
∠1 is rightm∠1 = 90°Def right angle
∠ABC is straightm∠ABC = 180°Straight Angle Postulate
ray BP goes through the inside of ∠ABCm∠ABP + m∠PBC = m∠ABCAngle Addition Postulate (AAP)
∠1 and ∠2 form a linear pair(words) they are side by side and their outside rays make a lineDef linear pair
∠1 and ∠2 form a linear pair∠1 and ∠2 are supplementaryLPT (once proved)

And the algebra reasons: Substitution (swap in something equal), Transitivity (if a = b and b = c, then a = c), Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division (do the same thing to both sides).

The T Theorem

If two angles of equal measure form a linear pair, then each is right. Picture a ray standing straight up from a line, like the letter T upside down.

A recipe when you're stuck

  1. Write the Given as line 1.
  2. Look at the Given and ask: what does each word mean? Write that down with “Def ___.”
  3. Turn the words into equations (see the translation table).
  4. Look at the last line you need (the Prove). What equation would give you that? Work toward it with algebra.
  5. Turn the final equation back into words with a definition.

#2 Converse LPT

This is the LPT run backwards. Given: adjacent ∠ACD and ∠BCD are supplementary. Prove: they form a linear pair.

Hint 1: the plan

Supplementary → the two angles add to 180° → the big angle ∠ACB is 180° → so it's straight → so rays CA and CB make a line → so it's a linear pair.

Hint 2: the reasons, in order

Given → Def supplementary → Angle Addition Postulate (m∠ACD + m∠BCD = m∠ACB) → Transitivity (m∠ACB = 180°) → Straight Angle Postulate (∠ACB is straight) → Def straight angle (rays CA and CB form a line) → Def linear pair.

#3 The T Theorem

This is Model proof 2 above. Try it from memory.

#4 Complement Theorem
Hint

Copy the LPT proof, but the big angle is right (90°) instead of straight (180°). Reasons: Given → Def right angle (m∠BAC = 90°) → Angle Addition Postulate (m∠BAD + m∠DAC = m∠BAC) → Transitivity (= 90°) → Def complementary.

#5 Same Supplements Theorem (SST)

Given: ∠A and ∠B are each supplementary to ∠C. Prove: m∠A = m∠B.

Hint

Write two equations: m∠A + m∠C = 180° and m∠B + m∠C = 180° (Def supplementary). Subtract m∠C from both sides of each (Subtraction). Now m∠A and m∠B both equal 180° − m∠C, so they equal each other (Transitivity).

#6 Same Complements Theorem (SCT)
Hint

Exactly the same as the SST, but with 90° instead of 180°. Write your own Given and Prove by copying the SST's and changing the word.

#7 Vertical Angles Theorem (VAT)
Hint 1

Use the X picture above. You want to prove m∠1 = m∠3. Notice that ∠2 sits between them: ∠1 and ∠2 are a linear pair, and ∠2 and ∠3 are a linear pair.

Hint 2

Short proof: (1) ∠1 and ∠3 are vertical, Given. (2) ∠1, ∠2 form a linear pair and ∠2, ∠3 form a linear pair, Def linear pair. (3) ∠1 and ∠3 are both supplementary to ∠2, LPT. (4) m∠1 = m∠3, SST.

#8 Perpendicular Lines Theorem (PLT)

The definition of perpendicular only promises one right angle. You have to prove all four.

Hint

∠1 and ∠2 are a linear pair (Def linear pair), so they're supplementary (LPT), so m∠1 + m∠2 = 180° (Def supplementary). m∠1 = 90° (Def right angle). Substitute: 90° + m∠2 = 180°. Subtract: m∠2 = 90°. So ∠2 is right. Then do the same trick to get from ∠2 to ∠3, and from ∠3 to ∠4.

#9 Rays PQ and PR form a line
Hint 1

You want to show ∠2 and ∠3 form a linear pair. The Converse LPT (#2) does that if you can show they're supplementary.

Hint 2

∠1 and ∠2 form a linear pair, so m∠1 + m∠2 = 180°. Since m∠1 = m∠3, substitute to get m∠3 + m∠2 = 180°. So ∠2 and ∠3 are supplementary and adjacent → Converse LPT → linear pair → their outside rays PQ and PR form a line (Def linear pair).

#10 Outside angles of triangle KLM add to 360°
Hint (your teacher's questions)

What is the sum of the two angles in a linear pair? So what is the sum of all six angles in the three linear pairs? What is that total minus 180°?

Hint 2

Write m∠1 + m∠4 = 180°, m∠2 + m∠5 = 180°, m∠3 + m∠6 = 180° (LPT, Def supplementary). Add all three (Addition): the six angles total 540°. You're given m∠1 + m∠2 + m∠3 = 180°. Substitute and subtract 180° from both sides.

Worksheet Part II

D, P, or T?

  • D (definition): it just says what a word means. Look it up in the definitions list.
  • P (postulate): something we agree is true without proving it. There are only a few. In this chapter the angle one is the Straight Angle Postulate.
  • T (theorem): something we proved, like the LPT, VAT, T Theorem, and PLT.

Your teacher's key warning: a true fact about a thing is not automatically its definition. “Vertical angles are equal” is true, but it's a theorem (the VAT). The definition of vertical angles only says what they look like.

Use the Reasons list below to check each one. Watch the trap in #11 and #12: one right angle is the definition of perpendicular. Four right angles is the theorem.

3.4

Part–Whole Proofs

Class lecture video3.4 worksheet (PDF)
Big ideaThe pieces add up to the whole. Write the “pieces add up” equation using the Segment Addition Postulate or the Angle Addition Postulate, then finish with ordinary algebra.

Words you'll need

  • Midpoint: C is the midpoint of AB when C is between A and B and AC = CB. (Reason: Def midpoint.)
  • Bisects: ray BD bisects ∠ABC when it goes through the inside and splits it into two equal angles. (Reason: Def angle bisector.)
  • Greater than: p > q when p = q + t for some positive t. Example: 12 > 7 because 12 = 7 + 5. (Reason: Def greater than.)
  • Less than: p < q when p = q − t for some positive t.
  • Every length and every angle measure is positive. (Reason: Ruler Postulate for lengths, Protractor Postulate for angles.)

#1 Halfway Theorem

This is the model proof above. Practice it from memory.

#2 Equal Wholes, Equal Halves (EWEH)
Hint

Use the Halfway Theorem twice: AC = ½AB and DF = ½DE. You're given AB = DE, so multiply both sides by ½ (Multiplication): ½AB = ½DE. Then chain them together with Substitution or Transitivity to get AC = DF.

#3 Many Parts Theorem (MPT)

Picture A — C — D — B on one segment.

Hint

Use the SAP twice. First on the small piece: AC + CD = AD. Then on the big piece: AD + DB = AB. Substitute the first into the second: AC + CD + DB = AB.

#4 Equal Parts, Equal Wholes (EPEW)
Hint

SAP on each segment: AE + EB = AB and CF + FD = CD. Since AE = CF and EB = FD, substitute into the first equation to get CF + FD = AB. Now AB and CD both equal CF + FD, so AB = CD (Transitivity).

#5 Whole Minus Part (WMP)
Hint

SAP on each: AE + EB = AB and CF + FD = CD. Subtract to get each missing piece alone: EB = AB − AE and FD = CD − CF. Since AB = CD and AE = CF, substitute so both right sides match. Then EB = FD.

#6 Whole > Part

You make the diagram and the Given/Prove yourself. Use the A — C — B picture above.

Hint 1

Given: C is between A and B. Prove: AB > AC and AB > CB.

Hint 2

SAP gives AB = AC + CB. CB is a length, so it's positive (Ruler Postulate). That's exactly the pattern “p = q + (something positive),” so AB > AC by Def greater than. Do the same for CB.

Worksheet Part II · just read these two

Linear Tuples Theorem (LTT): any number of side-by-side angles that make a straight line add up to 180°.

Full Circle Theorem (FCT): any number of side-by-side angles that go all the way around a point add up to 360°.

You don't prove these, but you will use them as reasons.

#6 Four equal angles means perpendicular

#7 Converse VAT
Hint

3.5

Indirect Proof

Class lecture video3.5 worksheet (PDF)
Big ideaPretend the opposite of what you want is true. Show that this leads to something impossible. So the opposite must be false, and what you wanted is true.

Think of a detective

“Suppose the butler did it. Then he was in the kitchen at 9pm. But the butler was on camera at the train station at 9pm. That's impossible. So the butler didn't do it.”

That's an indirect proof. The detective assumed the opposite, found a contradiction, and threw the assumption away.

GivenWhat you're told.
ProveWhat you want.
AssumptionThe opposite of Prove.
ReductionReason step by step until something clashes with a fact you know.
Conclusion“So the Assumption is false,” and restate the Prove.

Worked example · from class

Given: ∠A and ∠B are supplementary.
Prove: Not both ∠A and ∠B are obtuse.
Assumption: Both ∠A and ∠B are obtuse.
Reduction: Obtuse angles measure more than 90°. So m∠A + m∠B is more than 180°. But the Given says they're supplementary, so their sum is exactly 180°. That's a contradiction.
Conclusion: The Assumption is false. Not both ∠A and ∠B are obtuse.

Notice the Reduction is written in sentences, not a table. That's called a paragraph proof. You still give a reason for each step.

Getting the Assumption right: how to say the opposite

Prove says…Assumption says…
n is oddn is even (not odd)
Both are acuteAt least one is not acute
Not both are obtuseBoth are obtuse
Neither one is straightAt least one is straight
They meet in at most one pointThey meet in two or more points
X went firstX did not go first

Where can the contradiction come from? The Given, a definition, a postulate, or a theorem. Say which one: “This contradicts the Given.”

Your teacher did #1. For each of the others, follow the same shape. Read the word only carefully: “Fred shaves only people who don't shave themselves” means if Fred shaves you, then you don't shave yourself.

#2 Fred does not shave himself
Hint

Assumption: Fred shaves himself. Fred lives in Muncie, so he's one of the people Fred shaves. By the “only” rule, that person doesn't shave himself. Contradiction!

#3 Fred does not live in Muncie
Hint

Assumption: Fred lives in Muncie. Then either Fred shaves himself or he doesn't. Check both cases. If he does, the “only” rule says he doesn't (like #2). If he doesn't, the “all” rule says he does (like #1). Either way you get a contradiction.

Hint

3.6

Two Truths and a Lie

3.6 worksheet (PDF)GeoGebra Geometry (free)
Big ideaThis is counterexample hunting (from 3.1) with a computer. Build the Given in GeoGebra so it can't break, then drag points around until the Conclusion fails. That picture is your counterexample.

Step by step for each statement

  1. Find the Given (after “if”) and the Conclusion (after “then”).
  2. Build the Given in GeoGebra using the tools, not by eye. If two sides must be equal, use a circle so they stay equal when you drag. If a ray must bisect, use the Angle Bisector tool.
  3. Use the Distance/Length and Angle tools to show the numbers the Conclusion talks about.
  4. Drag points around. Watch the numbers.
  5. If the Conclusion stays true no matter what, it's probably one of the truths. If you can make it false even once, you found the lie. Screenshot it.

Building useful shapes in GeoGebra

  • Triangle with AB = BC: put point B, draw a circle centered at B through A, then put C anywhere on the circle. Now BA = BC no matter how you drag.
  • Two triangles with a matching side: draw segment AB, then use “Segment with Given Length” to make RS the same length.
  • Matching angle: use the “Angle with Given Size” tool to copy an angle's measure.
  • Right triangle: draw a segment, then “Perpendicular Line” through one endpoint, and put the third corner on that line.

What does “congruent” mean?

Two shapes are congruent when they are exact copies: every side and every angle matches its partner. For triangles ABC and RST that means AB = RS, BC = ST, AC = RT, and m∠A = m∠R, m∠B = m∠S, m∠C = m∠T.

To show a “congruent” statement is a lie, make two shapes that fit the Given but are clearly different sizes or shapes.

Bonus slides in 3.6: two famous indirect proofs

There are infinitely many primes (Euclid). Assume there's a last prime. Multiply every prime together and add 1. Call it S. S is bigger than every prime, so it isn't prime. Dividing S by any prime leaves remainder 1, so it isn't composite either. But every whole number 2 or bigger is prime or composite. Contradiction, so the primes never run out.

√2 is irrational (Pythagoras). Assume √2 = a/b, a fraction in lowest terms. Squaring gives a² = 2b², so a² is even, so a is even. Write a = 2k. Then 4k² = 2b², so b² = 2k², so b is even too. But then a/b wasn't in lowest terms. Contradiction.

Reasons list for Chapter 3

These are the things you're allowed to write in the Reason column. The wording comes from your class's official lists. Theorems are listed in the order they're proven, and you can only use a theorem after it has been proven.

Definitions

DefMidpoint
C is between A and B, and AC = CB.
DefAngle bisector
A ray through the inside of an angle that splits it into two equal angles.
DefRight angle
Measures 90°.
DefStraight angle
Its two sides form a line. (The definition does not say 180°. That's a postulate.)
DefAcute / Obtuse
Acute: less than 90°. Obtuse: more than 90° and less than 180°.
DefAdjacent angles
Share a side, and their insides don't overlap.
DefLinear pair
Adjacent angles whose non-shared sides form a line.
DefVertical angles
Opposite angles formed when two lines cross.
DefComplementary / Supplementary
Measures add to 90° / add to 180°. The angles don't have to touch.
DefPerpendicular lines
Two lines that form a right angle (just one!).
DefGreater than / Less than
p > q when p = q + t, and p < q when p = q − t, for some positive t.

Postulates

PostLine Existence Postulate
Through any two points there is exactly one line.
PostRuler Postulate
Every segment has a positive length.
PostSegment Addition Postulate (SAP)
If C is between A and B, then AC + CB = AB.
PostProtractor Postulate
Every angle has a positive measure.
PostAngle Addition Postulate (AAP)
If ray BP goes through the inside of ∠ABC, then m∠ABP + m∠PBC = m∠ABC.
PostStraight Angle Postulate
Straight angles measure 180°, and angles that measure 180° are straight.
PostAlgebra
Reflexivity, Transitivity, Substitution, Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division, Distribution.

Equal wholes minus equal parts leave equal parts.

Angles all the way around a point add to 360°.
